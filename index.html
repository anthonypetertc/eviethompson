Job Market Paper

Money, Time, and the Demand for Children

with Guy Michaels

Paper coming soon

Presented at: London Family Economics Workshop; LSE Labour Economics Work in Progress Seminar

Abstract

US fertility has fallen well below replacement, even as stated fertility ideals have not. We ask how much money and time women are willing to trade off for additional children, a question that is difficult to answer using observational data or even natural experiments. We field a discrete choice experiment in which 1,995 women choose between hypothetical futures varying the number of additional children, household income, and time use. Demand falls with both income and time prices, and rises with income faster than proportionally: larger families are a luxury good. Exploratory results show that full-time work, but not part-time work, is perceived as costly, and that baseline demand varies by age, religiosity, and desired fertility even as price responses vary less. Strikingly, most women require compensation, often significant, to accept a second additional child, including many who say they want two more. This finding complicates the standard reading of a stated ideal of two children as evidence of unmet demand: once its price is held fixed, we find little demand to reach it.

Work in Progress

Replication as a Form of Deterrence

Abstract

The use of replication in science is crucial for checking the validity of research methods and results. This paper demonstrates another use of replication, as a form of deterrence against low-quality research. I propose a principal-agent model of a journal that publishes a researcher’s paper. The journal induces research effort by publishing replications of the researcher’s work, introducing a risk that the researcher is caught if they perform bad quality research. If caught, the researcher incurs a reputational cost. When this cost is high, the journal’s strategy is more effective and they are able to induce higher effort levels. I also find that the optimal strategy for the journal is to only publish replications that overturn the results of the original study. Finally, when the researcher knows before submitting that their work is poor quality, I characterise the conditions under which the journal chooses to publish enough replications to deter them from submitting.
